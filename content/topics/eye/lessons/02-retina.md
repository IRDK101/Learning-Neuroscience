## A three-cell chain {#chain}

::diagram{id="retina" highlight="cone,bipolar-cell,ganglion-cell" caption="Light comes in from the top."}

The retina's main route is three cells long. [[Photoreceptors|photoreceptor]] absorb light and pass the signal to [[bipolar cells|bipolar-cell]], which pass it to [[ganglion cells|ganglion-cell]]. Ganglion-cell axons gather at the optic disk to form the optic nerve.

Photoreceptors and bipolar cells are neurons, but they use **graded potentials**. Ganglion cells are the only cells in the chain that fire action potentials.

::focus{highlight="horizontal-cell,amacrine-cell"}

Two kinds of cells work sideways. [[Horizontal cells|horizontal-cell]] adjust the photoreceptor-to-bipolar synapse, and [[amacrine cells|amacrine-cell]] adjust the bipolar-to-ganglion synapse.

::check{q="q-eye-spikes"}

## Inside out {#inverted}

::focus{highlight="pigmented-epithelium,rod,cone"}

The retina is [[built backwards|inverted-retina]]. The photoreceptors are at the very back, so light must pass through the ganglion and bipolar layers to reach them. Behind the photoreceptors, the dark [[pigmented epithelium|pigmented-epithelium]] absorbs any light they miss, so it doesn't bounce around and blur the image. At the fovea, the upper layers are pushed aside to give light a clear path.

## Rods and cones {#rods-cones}

- [[Rods|rod]] are very sensitive to light, so they work in dim light, but they don't see color and give little detail.
- [[Cones|cone]] need more light but see color and detail. There are three types, most sensitive to blue, green or red light. Colors are mixtures of their responses. Missing or faulty cone types cause [[color blindness|color-blindness]].

Cones are packed into the fovea, and there are no rods there. Rods take over just outside it. Cones still occur across the whole retina, just much more sparsely, which is why you still see some color off to the side. That's also why a dim star is easier to see if you look slightly away from it.

::check{q="q-eye-rods"}

## Why the fovea is sharp {#convergence}

In the fovea, each cone has a private line: one cone → one bipolar cell → one ganglion cell. The brain knows exactly where the light landed. Elsewhere, many photoreceptors feed one ganglion cell ([[convergence]]). That makes vision more sensitive but blurrier, because the brain can't tell which of the receptors caught the light.

::check{q="q-eye-fovea"}

## Light turns photoreceptors off {#phototransduction}

Photoreceptors contain stacks of membrane disks filled with [[opsin|opsin]], the light-absorbing molecule.

::pathway{id="phototransduction"}

Surprisingly, photoreceptors are **depolarized in the dark** (about −30 mV), because Na⁺ channels stay open. Light starts a chain of reactions that **closes** those channels, so the cell **hyperpolarizes**. A photoreceptor releases glutamate all the time in the dark, and **less** glutamate in the light.

::check{q="q-eye-dark"}
