:::added
**New to this?** Taste and smell are the [[chemical-senses]]: they detect molecules that touch a sensory organ. They're among the oldest senses in evolution. Taste (gustation) works on molecules called [[tastants|tastant]]; smell (olfaction) works on airborne [[odorants|odorant]].
:::

## From mouth to throat {#anatomy}

Air and food share a path. The palate separates the mouth from the nasal cavity, but the two connect at the back of the throat. That's why smell matters so much for flavor. Below the throat, the trachea (in front) goes to the lungs and the esophagus (behind) goes to the stomach. The [[epiglottis]] closes over the trachea when you swallow.

## Papillae and taste buds {#taste-buds}

::diagram{id="taste-bud" highlight="taste-bud,papilla" caption="Taste cells reach up to the taste pore; axons wait below."}

The bumps on your tongue are [[papillae|papilla]]. They aren't receptors themselves: the [[taste buds|taste-bud]] sit in the grooves along their sides. Saliva dissolves food and carries tastants into each bud through its [[taste-pore]].

::focus{highlight="taste-cell,microvilli"}

Inside the bud, [[taste cells|taste-cell]] reach their [[microvilli]] up into the pore. Taste cells aren't spiking neurons. They respond with **graded** depolarization and release more transmitter the more tastant they detect, so the [[gustatory afferent axons|gustatory-afferent-axon]] below fire faster. A taste cell lives only about two weeks, because acid, heat and spice keep damaging it.

::focus{highlight="basal-cell"}

[[Basal cells|basal-cell]] at the bottom of the bud replace the taste cells and help pass signals on to the axons, a little like astrocytes.

Each taste cell has favorite tastes it responds to most strongly. Every bud mixes cells with different favorites, so **every part of the tongue can detect every taste**. The old "tongue map" is a myth.

::check{q="q-taste-map"}

## Salty and sour {#salty-sour}

::pathway{id="salt-transduction"}

**Salty** is mostly Na⁺. Sodium flows straight into the taste cell through channels that are always open, and the inside becomes less negative. That opens voltage-gated Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ channels near the base. Calcium makes vesicles release **serotonin** onto the gustatory axon.

**Sour** means acid, which means lots of H⁺ (protons). Protons enter through proton channels at the top of the cell. They also **block K⁺ channels** that are normally open. Positive charge comes in and stays in, so the cell depolarizes and releases serotonin just as in salty taste.

::check{q="q-taste-sour"}

## Sweet, bitter and umami {#gpcr}

These three use metabotropic, G-protein-coupled receptors instead of letting ions straight in.

::pathway{id="sweet-bitter-umami-transduction"}

- [[Sweet and umami receptors|taste-receptor-dimer]] are each built from two subunits: one pairing for sweet, another for umami.
- [[Bitter|bitter-receptors]] uses a whole family of about 25 different receptors. Many poisons taste bitter, so it pays to detect a wide range of them.

When a tastant binds, a G protein switches on [[phospholipase C|phospholipase-c]], which turns PIP2 into [[ip3]]. IP3 releases Ca²⁺ from stores inside the cell and opens a Na⁺ channel. The cell depolarizes, and calcium opens channels that let [[ATP|atp-transmitter]] out. Here ATP itself is the transmitter that excites the gustatory axon.

::check{q="q-taste-atp"}

## To the brain {#pathway}

::pathway{id="taste-pathway"}

Gustatory axons travel in three cranial nerves to the [[gustatory-nucleus]] in the medulla, then to the [[vpm-nucleus]] of the thalamus, and finally to the [[gustatory-cortex]] in the insula.

## Learning what made you sick {#aversion}

Rats given a new food and then made nauseous avoid that food afterwards. Rats that didn't get sick eat more of it next time. This is [[conditioned-taste-aversion]] (the Garcia effect), and one pairing is enough.

Not every pairing is this easy. Taste links readily with sickness and sound with pain, but taste–pain and sound–sickness links are hard to learn. Evolution has tuned what we learn to what kept our ancestors alive.
