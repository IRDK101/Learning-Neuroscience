## Symptoms {#symptoms}

[[Schizophrenia|schizophrenia]] is a severe disorder of disconnection from reality that disrupts thought, perception, mood and movement. It usually emerges in late adolescence or the twenties, sometimes earlier. Its symptoms fall into two groups ([[positive and negative symptoms|positive-negative-symptoms]]):

- **Positive symptoms** are things present that shouldn't be: hallucinations (often hearing voices, sometimes so loud that real sounds are drowned out) and delusions. They're usually noticed first.
- **Negative symptoms** are things missing that should be there: trouble with planning, memory, attention and motivation. They're often the most disabling.

## Genes and environment {#causes}

Schizophrenia runs in families: the closer an affected relative, the higher your risk. But genes act together with the environment ([[genes and environment|gene-environment-interaction]]).

A clear example involves [[COMT|comt]], an enzyme that breaks down dopamine. A common variant comes in two forms, Val and Met. In a long-term study, cannabis use during adolescence raised the risk of psychosis most in people with two Val copies, somewhat in people with one, and not at all in people with two Met copies. Neither the gene nor the drug alone told the story.

On average, people with schizophrenia also have **enlarged lateral ventricles**, though not consistently enough to diagnose with.

::check{q="q-mi-comt"}

## Dopamine {#dopamine}

The [[dopamine hypothesis|dopamine-hypothesis]] says too much dopamine signaling drives psychosis. Evidence:

- [[Antipsychotics|antipsychotics]] block dopamine receptors, and the more tightly a drug binds them, the lower the dose needed.
- [[Amphetamine|amphetamine]], which raises dopamine, can cause brief psychotic symptoms.

Many antipsychotics have serious side effects on movement and on reward, because they block dopamine everywhere, including the nigrostriatal pathway.

## Glutamate {#glutamate}

Drugs that block NMDA glutamate receptors, [[PCP and ketamine|pcp-ketamine]], can cause episodes resembling schizophrenia, with both positive and negative symptoms. This supports the [[glutamate (NMDA) hypothesis|nmda-hypofunction]]: reduced NMDA receptor function contributes to the disorder. Newer treatments aimed at glutamate hope to avoid the side effects of dopamine blockers.
