## Loewi's two hearts {#loewi}

How does one neuron pass its message to the next? Galvani showed nerves are electrical. In 1921, **Otto Loewi** showed there's chemistry too. He kept two frog hearts in fluid and stimulated the **vagus nerve** of the first, which slowed it. When fluid from the first heart reached the second, the second heart slowed too, even though its nerve was never touched. Something *chemical* had carried the message. (It turned out to be acetylcholine.)

## Electrical synapses {#electrical}

At an **electrical synapse**, or **gap junction**, the two cells are directly connected by channels, so ions flow straight from one into the next. Each channel is a pair of **connexons**, one from each cell, and each connexon is built from six **connexin** subunits.

- They're **bidirectional**: signals can go either way.
- They're **very fast**, faster than chemical synapses.
- But each signal is **weak**: one action potential barely changes the next cell's voltage.
- They're useful for synchronizing large networks, and more common in fetuses and young children.

## Chemical synapses {#chemical}

::diagram{id="synapse" highlight="presynaptic-terminal,synaptic-cleft,postsynaptic-dendrite"}

Most synapses are **chemical**. The **presynaptic** cell releases neurotransmitter from vesicles into the **synaptic cleft**, and receptors on the **postsynaptic** cell detect it. The message starts electrical (the action potential), turns chemical in the synapse, then turns electrical again. Chemical synapses work in one direction only.
