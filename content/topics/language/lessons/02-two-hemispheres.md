## A left-sided skill {#lateralization}

In most people, language depends mostly on the **left** hemisphere ([[lateralization of language|language-lateralization]]). The anatomy differs too: the [[planum temporale|planum-temporale]], the core of Wernicke's area, is usually larger on the left, and the left lateral fissure tends to be longer and flatter.

Language may share the left hemisphere with handedness. Most people are right-handed (controlled by the left hemisphere), and both writing and speech need very fine motor control. Left-handers are more likely to have language on the right or on both sides.

## The Wada test {#wada}

Before brain surgery, doctors sometimes need to know which side handles language. In the [[Wada test|wada-test]], a short-acting anesthetic is injected into one carotid artery, putting that hemisphere to sleep for a few minutes. In most people, silencing the left hemisphere stops speech. A minority are bilateral or right-dominant, more often left-handers. Brain scans agree: language tasks activate the left side more, on average, though the right still contributes.

## Split brains {#split-brain}

::diagram{id="brain-midsagittal" highlight="corpus-callosum" caption="Cutting the corpus callosum separates the hemispheres."}

To stop severe seizures spreading, surgeons sometimes cut the [[corpus callosum|corpus-callosum]]. Afterwards the two hemispheres can't share information ([[split-brain studies|split-brain]]).

- A word flashed to the **right** visual field reaches the **left** hemisphere, which can read it aloud.
- A word flashed to the **left** visual field reaches only the **right** hemisphere. The person **can't say it**, because speech is on the left, but the right hemisphere understands enough to **draw** it or **pick out the object** with the left hand.
- An object felt out of sight with the left hand can't be named, for the same reason.

## Sign language and second languages {#sign}

[[Sign languages|sign-language-brain]] use the same left-hemisphere language areas as spoken language when learned early, even though they're visual. Languages learned later in life, signed or spoken, tend to engage both hemispheres more. That may be why brain damage sometimes spares a second language.

Stimulating the cortex during surgery while a patient talks ([[stimulation mapping|cortical-stimulation-mapping]]) shows that language sites vary a lot from person to person, and that one function can be spread across several areas.

## Does language shape thought? {#sapir-whorf}

The [[Sapir-Whorf hypothesis|sapir-whorf-hypothesis]] says language shapes thought. The strong version, that you can't think about something without a word for it, isn't supported. The weaker version has evidence: having a word makes you more likely to think in that category. Speakers of languages with gendered nouns, for example, tend to describe objects in ways that match the noun's grammatical gender.
