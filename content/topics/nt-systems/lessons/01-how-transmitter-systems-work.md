:::added
**New to this?** A neuron passes its message to the next cell at a **synapse**. The sending neuron releases a chemical messenger, a [[neurotransmitter]], into a tiny gap. The receiving cell has **receptors** that detect it. This topic is about the major transmitters: how each is made, where in the brain it comes from, how it's cleared away, and how drugs interfere.
:::

## The parts of a chemical synapse {#parts}

::diagram{id="synapse" highlight="presynaptic-terminal,synaptic-cleft,postsynaptic-dendrite" caption="Tap any part to see what it does."}

The **presynaptic terminal** is the end of the sending neuron's axon. It's packed with **synaptic vesicles**, little bubbles of transmitter. Across the **synaptic cleft** sits the **postsynaptic dendrite**, covered in receptors.

## Four steps at every chemical synapse {#four-steps}

::pathway{id="chemical-transmission-steps"}

::focus{highlight="synaptic-vesicle"}

**1. Synthesis and storage.** Small transmitters (amino acids like glutamate, and amines like dopamine) are made right in the axon terminal and loaded into vesicles. Big peptide transmitters are made near the cell body and shipped down the axon.

::focus{highlight="voltage-gated-calcium-channel,snare-proteins"}

**2. Release.** Vesicles are tied to the membrane by [[SNARE proteins|snare-proteins]]. When an action potential reaches the terminal, **voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels** open and calcium rushes in. The Ca²⁺ lets the SNAREs fuse the vesicles with the membrane, and transmitter spills into the cleft.

::focus{highlight="ionotropic-receptor,metabotropic-receptor,autoreceptor"}

**3. Receptor activation.** The transmitter binds receptors on the postsynaptic cell. The presynaptic cell has **autoreceptors** too, so it can "listen to itself" and adjust how much it releases.

::focus{highlight="reuptake-transporter,acetylcholinesterase,astrocyte"}

**4. Deactivation.** The transmitter has to be cleared so the synapse can signal again quickly. There are four ways:

- **Reuptake:** transporters pull it back into the presynaptic terminal to be recycled.
- **Enzymes** break it down.
- **Glia** (astrocytes) take it up.
- It **diffuses** away.

::check{q="q-nt-deactivation-ways"}

## The receptor decides the effect {#receptor-decides}

::focus{highlight="ionotropic-receptor,metabotropic-receptor,g-protein"}

You can't predict what a transmitter does from the transmitter alone. **The receptor it binds decides.**

- **Ionotropic receptors** are ion channels themselves. Transmitter binds, the channel opens, ions flow. This is fast.
- **Metabotropic receptors** activate **G proteins** inside the cell, which open channels or trigger second messengers. This is slower but can last longer and be more powerful.

Receptors that are slower to open are also slower to close, so their effects last longer. Acetylcholine, for example, excites some cells and inhibits others depending on the receptor.

::check{q="q-nt-receptor-decides"}

## Agonists and antagonists {#agonists-antagonists}

Drugs work because many are shaped enough like natural transmitters to fit the same binding sites.

- An [[agonist]] **mimics or boosts** a transmitter: it binds and activates the receptor, sometimes even more strongly than the real thing.
- An [[antagonist]] is close enough to fit the receptor but **doesn't activate it**. It just sits there and blocks the natural transmitter.

Some drugs don't touch the receptor at all. They block a transporter (like cocaine) or an enzyme (like MAO inhibitors). You'll meet these in the next lessons.

::check{q="q-nt-antagonist"}

## "-ergic" and diffuse modulatory systems {#diffuse}

::diagram{id="nt-overlay" layers="ach,da,ne,serotonin,deep" caption="Each color is one transmitter system. Toggle them in the map."}

Neurons are named for the transmitter they release: **cholinergic** (acetylcholine), **dopaminergic** (dopamine), **noradrenergic** (norepinephrine), **serotonergic** (serotonin).

Four of these form **diffuse modulatory systems**: a small cluster of neurons whose axons spread across huge areas of the brain. They don't carry a specific message so much as **tune** how many circuits behave at once: arousal, mood, attention, learning.

::focus{highlight="nucleus-basalis,medial-septal-nuclei,pmt-complex" layers="ach,deep"}

**Acetylcholine** comes from the basal forebrain and the PMT complex.

::focus{highlight="substantia-nigra,ventral-tegmental-area" layers="da,deep"}

**Dopamine** comes from the substantia nigra and the ventral tegmental area.

::focus{highlight="locus-coeruleus" layers="ne,deep"}

**Norepinephrine** comes from the locus coeruleus.

::focus{highlight="raphe-nuclei" layers="serotonin,deep"}

**Serotonin** comes from the raphe nuclei.
