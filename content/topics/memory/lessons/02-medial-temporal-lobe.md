## Memories as networks {#engram}

A memory isn't stored in one cell. It's thought to be an [[engram]]: a network of cells that fire together, and any cell can belong to many engrams. The network is built by [[Hebb's rule|hebbian-learning]]: neurons that fire together wire together. Each time an experience activates the same cells, their connections grow stronger. Later, seeing just part of the scene can switch on part of the network, which then reactivates the rest and brings back the whole memory.

## H.M. {#hm}

::diagram{id="medial-temporal-lobe" highlight="hippocampus" caption="The memory structures of the medial temporal lobe."}

In 1953, Henry Molaison ([[H.M.|hm-henry-molaison]]) had much of both medial temporal lobes, including the hippocampi, removed to stop severe seizures. The seizures improved, but he could no longer form new declarative memories, a severe **[[anterograde amnesia|anterograde-amnesia]]**. He worked with researchers for about half a century without ever recognizing them. He also lost some memories from before surgery (**[[retrograde amnesia|retrograde-amnesia]]**).

Other abilities survived. His working memory and older memories were intact, and he could still learn new **skills**, improving with practice while having no memory of practicing. Forming new declarative memories needs the [[medial temporal lobe|medial-temporal-lobe]]; skills don't.

[[Alzheimer's disease|alzheimers-disease]] starts in this same region and spreads, which is why declarative memory fails early.

## Inside the medial temporal lobe {#mtl}

::focus{highlight="entorhinal-cortex,perirhinal-cortex,parahippocampal-cortex"}

The [[hippocampus]] is tightly connected to the cortex around it: the [[entorhinal cortex|entorhinal-cortex]], its gateway, plus the [[perirhinal cortex|perirhinal-cortex]] and the [[parahippocampal cortex|parahippocampal-cortex]].

In the [[delayed non-match to sample|delayed-non-match-to-sample]] task, a monkey sees an object, then after a delay sees it again next to a new one, and finds food under the new one. Healthy monkeys stay accurate even after long delays. With medial temporal damage, they do fine at short delays but get worse as the delay grows, which points to a problem forming lasting memories. Damage to the cortex around the hippocampus hurts more than damage to the hippocampus alone.

This task tests **recognition**: have I seen this before? Detailed **recall** of an event is different, and the two rely on different parts of the medial temporal lobe ([[recognition vs recall|recognition-vs-recall]]). Perirhinal damage impairs recognition, while hippocampal damage impairs recalling the details of events more than recognizing things.

::check{q="q-mem-recognition"}

## Consolidation {#consolidation}

How does a new memory become permanent? In the standard model of [[consolidation]]:

1. At first, the hippocampus binds together the pieces of the memory stored around the neocortex, and recall depends on it.
2. Over time, the cortical network strengthens until it can stand on its own and no longer needs the hippocampus (systems consolidation).

That fits H.M.: old memories survived while new ones couldn't form. The rival [[multiple trace theory|multiple-trace-theory]] says each recall lays down a new trace, and that detailed episodic memories always need the hippocampus.
