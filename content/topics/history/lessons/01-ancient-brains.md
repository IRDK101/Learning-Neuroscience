## Trepanation {#trepanation}

People were operating on the head long before writing. **Trepanation**, drilling a hole in a living person's skull, goes back to around 6,500 BCE and turns up on several continents. Some of these skulls show bone that healed afterward, so patients survived. Was it religious, or an attempt to cure something wrong with the brain? Nobody knows, but it may be the earliest form of surgery.

## The first written record {#papyrus}

The **Edwin Smith Papyrus** from ancient Egypt, written around 1600 BCE {{C-001}}, describes 48 medical cases, mostly injuries to the head and brain. Its author, a physician, was possibly an early neurologist. It even has hieroglyphs for the brain, its fluid, its folds and its membranes.

## The Greeks: brain or heart? {#greeks}

- **Hippocrates** (460–370 BCE), the "father of medicine", correctly placed intelligence and sensation in the brain. He also taught that health depends on balancing four body fluids (the humors).
- **Plato** agreed the brain was the seat of intelligence, but for the wrong reason: the head is the body's highest point, closest to the heavens. His *tripartite soul* split behavior into reason, spirit and appetite. All three turn out to be the brain's work.
- **Aristotle** went backward. He put the mind in the heart and thought the brain's job was to cool the blood. This idea lasted for centuries.

## Galen {#galen}

**Galen**, a Greek physician working in Rome in the 2nd century CE {{C-002}}, was a surgeon to gladiators. He noticed that men with head injuries behaved differently, and connected injury to changes in behavior, memory and function. He dissected animals. He still believed nerves pumped fluid around the body, but his link between brain damage and behavior was real progress.
