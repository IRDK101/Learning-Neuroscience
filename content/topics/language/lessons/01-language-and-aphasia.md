## A human universal {#universal}

[[Language|language]] draws on many systems: hearing or seeing (or touch, for Braille), memory and meaning, and fine motor control of more than 100 muscles in the mouth and throat, or of the hands for writing and signing. About 7,000 languages are spoken today, most of them in Asia and Africa, and every known human culture has language.

Children pick up whatever language surrounds them, apparently without effort. But there's a [[critical period|critical-period]]: children who aren't exposed to language in their first few years struggle to acquire any language later.

Other animals communicate, and some can learn signs, but a few features seem uniquely human ([[what makes human language special|human-language-features]]):

- words for **abstract** ideas with no direct sensory meaning,
- **[[syntax]]**, rules for ordering words that build layered meanings,
- **counterfactuals**: talking about things that never happened, the distant past or future, or what might be.

## Two classic aphasias {#aphasias}

::diagram{id="language-areas" highlight="brocas-area,wernickes-area" caption="Arrows show the route for repeating a word you hear."}

An [[aphasia]] is a loss of language after brain damage. Aphasias were described as early as the 1770s. In the late 1800s, before brain scans, Broca and Wernicke tied them to brain areas by examining their patients' brains after death.

- In **[[Broca's aphasia|brocas-aphasia]]**, damage to [[Broca's area|brocas-area]] (in the frontal lobe, just in front of the face area of M1) disrupts **syntax**: speech is slow and halting and sentences are hard to assemble, but meaning and understanding are largely kept.
- In **[[Wernicke's aphasia|wernickes-aphasia]]**, damage to [[Wernicke's area|wernickes-area]] (in the temporal lobe, just behind A1) disrupts **[[semantics]]**: speech flows fluently, but the words don't make sense, and understanding is poor.

::check{q="q-lang-broca"}

## The Wernicke-Geschwind model {#model}

::focus{highlight="primary-auditory-cortex,wernickes-area,arcuate-fasciculus,brocas-area"}

::pathway{id="wernicke-geschwind-model"}

To repeat a word you hear: sound reaches A1, Wernicke's area works out its meaning, the [[arcuate fasciculus|arcuate-fasciculus]] carries it forward to Broca's area, which plans the sentence, and the face area of M1 moves the mouth. The arcuate fasciculus runs in both directions, which lets you listen and form your reply. This is the **Wernicke-Geschwind model**: oversimplified, but a useful map.

## Conduction aphasia {#conduction}

Damage to the arcuate fasciculus disconnects Wernicke's area from Broca's area. People with [[conduction aphasia|conduction-aphasia]] understand speech and speak fluently, but they **can't repeat** what they hear, especially nonsense words. They also make paraphasias, substituting a wrong word ("week" for "month") or a wrong-sounding one.

Wernicke predicted this syndrome from his model before anyone had seen a patient with it.
