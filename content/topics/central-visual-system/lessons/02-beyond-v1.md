## Many visual areas {#areas}

::diagram{id="brain-lateral" highlight="occipital-lobe,temporal-lobe,parietal-lobe" caption="Visual areas spread forward from the occipital lobe."}

V1 is only the start. From V1, information goes to V2, V3, V4, V5 and many more ([[visual association cortex|visual-association-cortex]]), which spread into the temporal and parietal lobes. Each area specializes in some feature: V4 matters for color, V5 for motion. Damage to one area can remove one part of vision and leave the rest.

## When one part goes {#deficits}

- **[[Akinetopsia|akinetopsia]]**, motion blindness: smooth motion looks like a series of still frames. It comes from damage to [[area V5|area-v5]] (MT), near the border of the occipital and temporal lobes.
- **[[Prosopagnosia|prosopagnosia]]**, face blindness: a face is still seen as a face, with eyes, nose and mouth, but the parts don't come together into a person you recognize. It comes from damage to the [[fusiform face area|fusiform-face-area]] on the underside of the temporal lobe.

::check{q="q-vis-face"}

Problems recognizing objects are called **object agnosias**:

- **[[Simultagnosia|simultagnosia]]**: only one object can be seen at a time, and overlapping objects can't be pulled apart. Damage to the [[posterior parietal cortex|posterior-parietal-cortex]].
- **[[Associative agnosia|associative-agnosia]]**: the patient can see an object's shape and even copy it but doesn't know what it is (they can draw a key without recognizing it as a key). Touching it still works. Damage to the temporal lobe near the fusiform face area.
- **[[Apperceptive agnosia|apperceptive-agnosia]]**: the patient can't perceive contours and edges, so they can neither recognize nor copy the object. Damage to V1 or V2.
