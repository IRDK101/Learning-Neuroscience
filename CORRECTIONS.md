- **Corrections (C-xxx):** the notes were clearly wrong or garbled. The site uses the fixed version, marks it `source: corrected`, and shows a "Corrected" badge with your original wording.
- **Flags (F-xx):** debatable or unverifiable points, or places where the lecture may have used a deliberate simplification. **The site keeps your notes' version** and attaches a short note. Tell me if any should become corrections.

## Corrections

### Exam 1: Foundations

**C-001 · History › Edwin Smith Papyrus**
- Notes: "discovered around 1650–1550 BCE … first medical document in human history altogether"
- Fixed: *Written* around 1600 BCE (probably copied from an older text) and bought by Edwin Smith in 1862. One of the **oldest known** surgical/medical texts.
- Why: The date is when it was written, not discovered. Other Egyptian medical papyri (e.g., Kahun, ~1800 BCE) are older.

**C-002 · History › Galen**
- Notes: "Galen (still Greece)"
- Fixed: A Greek physician who worked in **Rome** (2nd century CE), including as a surgeon to gladiators.
- Why: Place and era.

**C-003 · History › Descartes**
- Notes: "Renaissance era! … pineal gland … dictates hormones but isn't really that important"
- Fixed: **17th century** (early modern era). The pineal gland secretes **melatonin**, which regulates sleep–wake rhythms. It is not the seat of the mind, but it does have a real job.
- Why: Era, and an understated function.

**C-004 · History › Brodmann**
- Notes: "Korbinian Brodmann (1869–1918)"
- Fixed: **1868**–1918. He mapped 52 areas by **cytoarchitecture**: differences in cell types, density and layering.
- Why: Birth year; "different numbers of cells" undersells the method.

**C-005 · History › Golgi stain**
- Notes: "only stained neurons"
- Fixed: Stains a **small random fraction** of neurons, but stains each one **completely** (soma, dendrites, axon). That's why individual cells could be seen.
- Why: The point of the stain is that it is sparse.

**C-006 · Structure › Autonomic NS**
- Notes: "Sympathetic Branch involves fight or flight reactions (inflammatory, active functions)"
- Fixed: Fight-or-flight **arousal**: raised heart rate, dilated pupils, energy mobilized.
- Why: "Inflammatory" is not a defining feature of the sympathetic branch.

**C-007 · Structure › Optic chiasm**
- Notes: "signals from each eye travel to the opposite side … of the brain"
- Fixed: Only axons from the **nasal half** of each retina cross. Temporal-half axons stay on the same side. Each hemisphere ends up seeing the **opposite visual hemifield** with both eyes.
- Why: Partial decussation (also see C-042).

**C-008 · Structure › Meninges**
- Notes: "Pia Mater … creates blood/brain barrier"
- Fixed: The pia is the thin innermost layer hugging the brain. The **blood–brain barrier** is formed by tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells, supported by astrocytes.
- Why: Wrong structure.

**C-009 · Structure › Forebrain**
- Notes: "The Forebrain is the front area of your brain"
- Fixed: The forebrain (prosencephalon) is the **cerebrum plus the diencephalon** (thalamus, hypothalamus). It's the largest division, not just the front.
- Why: Developmental division, not a position.

**C-010 · Structure › Grey matter**
- Notes: "Grey Matter contains unmyelinated axons"
- Fixed: Grey matter is mostly **neuron cell bodies, dendrites and synapses**, plus unmyelinated axons. White matter is mostly myelinated axons.
- Why: Cell bodies are the defining feature.

**C-011 · Structure › Colliculi**
- Notes: "Colliculi are small structures right behind the brainstem"
- Fixed: The colliculi form the **roof (tectum) of the midbrain**, on its dorsal surface. Superior colliculus = visual/orienting; inferior colliculus = auditory.
- Why: They are part of the brainstem.

**C-012 · Structure › Cauda equina**
- Notes: "the very bottom of the spinal cord that … ends off in a giant bundle of nerves"
- Fixed: The bundle of **spinal nerve roots** below the point where the cord itself ends (around L1–L2).
- Why: It's the nerve roots, not cord tissue.

**C-013 · Neurons › Cytoskeleton**
- Notes: "Neurofilaments … perpendicular-ish to axons"
- Fixed: Neurofilaments run **lengthwise** along the axon. Their side-arms set the spacing between filaments, which helps set axon width.
- Why: Orientation.

**C-014 · Membrane at rest › What sets the resting potential**
- Notes: "Neurons … work constantly to balance the voltage mostly using a Sodium-Potassium Pump"
- Fixed: The resting potential comes mainly from **K⁺ leaking out** through always-open K⁺ channels. The membrane at rest is most permeable to K⁺. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump **maintains the concentration gradients** that make this possible, and adds only a few mV directly.
- Why: Common misconception.

**C-015 · Action potential › Ion flow**
- Notes: "channels allowing sodium and potassium inside"
- Fixed: Na⁺ flows **in** (depolarization). K⁺ flows **out** (repolarization and undershoot).
- Why: Direction of K⁺.

**C-016 · Action potential › Na⁺ channel structure**
- Notes: "4 subunits clumped together form a pore"
- Fixed: A **single long protein with 4 domains** forms the pore. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels are the ones built from 4 separate subunits.
- Why: Structure.

**C-017 · Action potential › Summation**
- Notes: "Temporal Summation is a group of simultaneous messages from neurons … Spatial Summation is a combination of signals from close dendrites"
- Fixed:
  - **Temporal summation:** EPSPs from the **same synapse** arriving in rapid succession add up.
  - **Spatial summation:** EPSPs from **different synapses** at the same time add up.
- Why: The notes swap in "simultaneous" for temporal.

**C-018 · Synaptic transmission › Definition**
- Notes: "Neurotransmitters are any cell/chemical/neuron/molecule that gets passed between neurons"
- Fixed: A **chemical** released by a neuron that acts on a target cell's receptors.
- Why: Cells aren't transmitters.

**C-019 · Synaptic transmission › Deactivation**
- Notes: "Neurotransmitter Deactivation is the final step to end the AP … Glia grab NTs out of the Glial cells"
- Fixed: Deactivation ends the **synaptic signal** (the AP is already over). Glia take transmitter up **from the synaptic cleft**.
- Why: Wrong event; garbled location.

**C-020 · Synaptic transmission › G proteins**
- Notes: "G Proteins … can connect to ionotropic receptors and kick off their opening"
- Fixed: G proteins can open **G-protein-gated ion channels** (the "shortcut pathway") or activate enzymes that make second messengers. Ionotropic receptors are opened by the transmitter itself.
- Why: Terminology.

**C-021 · NT systems › Choline**
- Notes: "Choline, which is not produced in our bodies and must be ingested"
- Fixed: The body (liver) makes **some** choline, but not enough. Most comes from **diet**, and its supply limits the rate of ACh synthesis.
- Why: Overstated.

**C-022 · NT systems › Dopamine synthesis**
- Notes: "Dopamine is created by Tyrosine + L-dopa + one more step to get to DA"
- Fixed: Tyrosine →(tyrosine hydroxylase, rate-limiting)→ L-DOPA →(**DOPA decarboxylase**)→ dopamine.
- Why: The missing enzyme.

**C-023 · NT systems › ACh targets**
- Notes: "smooth muscle (muscles we don't have voluntary control over like the heart)"
- Fixed: ACh acts on **skeletal muscle** (neuromuscular junction) and, through the parasympathetic system, on **smooth muscle and cardiac muscle**. The heart is cardiac muscle, not smooth.
- Why: Muscle type (also see C-045).

### Exam 2: Senses & Movement

**C-024 · Taste › Papillae and taste cells**
- Notes: "receptors (Papillae) … Food sheds some matter (gustants) … Taste Cells … detecting odorants"
- Fixed: **Papillae** are the bumps on the tongue that **house** taste buds; they are not receptors. Taste cells detect **tastants**. Odorants are for smell.
- Why: Terminology.

**C-025 · Taste › Bitter receptors**
- Notes: "Receptors … are made up of two protein subunits … Bitter has many receptor combinations"
- Fixed: Sweet (T1R2 + T1R3) and umami (T1R1 + T1R3) are **two-subunit** receptors. Bitter uses **many different T2R receptors (~25)**, which lets it detect a wide range of toxins.
- Why: Bitter isn't built from combinations of the same two subunits.

**C-026 · Smell › Odorant receptors**
- Notes: "aka Golf Receptors … Cells only respond to one odorant … humans have fewer GOLF receptors"
- Fixed:
  - The receptors are **odorant receptor proteins** coupled to the G protein **G_olf**. Golf is the G protein, not the receptor.
  - Each receptor **cell** expresses **one receptor type**, but each receptor responds to **many odorants**, and each odorant activates several receptors.
  - Humans have fewer receptor **types** and a much smaller epithelium than dogs.
- Why: Naming and the one-to-one claim.

**C-027 · Smell › cAMP**
- Notes: "G proteins activate Adenylyl Cyclase Enzymes, which then activates ATP and synthesizes cAMP"
- Fixed: Adenylyl cyclase **converts ATP into cAMP**.
- Why: ATP is the substrate.

**C-028 · Auditory › Sound properties**
- Notes: "Sound properties are frequency and distance. Frequency is the time to complete one cycle"
- Fixed: The two properties are **frequency** (cycles per second, in Hz; heard as pitch) and **intensity/amplitude** (heard as loudness). The time for one cycle is the **period**, which is the inverse of frequency.
- Why: Definition.

**C-029 · Auditory › Cochlear fluid path**
- Notes: "Hole at Apex … where fluid can travel through the basilar membrane … The structure dividing top and bottom chambers is the Basilar Membrane"
- Fixed:
  - At the apex, fluid passes between the scala vestibuli and scala tympani through the **helicotrema**, not through a membrane.
  - **Reissner's membrane** separates the scala vestibuli from the scala media.
  - The **basilar membrane** separates the scala media from the scala tympani. The organ of Corti sits on the basilar membrane.
- Why: Anatomy.

**C-030 · Auditory › Outer hair cells**
- Notes: "Intensity/volume gets 'summarized' by outer hair cells"
- Fixed: About 95% of auditory nerve fibers carry signals from **inner** hair cells, which are the main sensors. **Outer hair cells act as a cochlear amplifier**: they change length and boost basilar-membrane motion, which sharpens sensitivity and frequency tuning.
- Why: Your notes marked this as a guess ("Maybe…"). The established function is different.

**C-031 · Auditory › Cochlear implants**
- Notes: "Later ear damage is usually caused by damage to the middle ear bones, which wouldn't be helped"
- Fixed: Most permanent hearing loss (from aging or noise) is **sensorineural**: hair cells are lost. That's exactly what cochlear implants bypass.
- Why: Reversed.

**C-032 · Auditory › Pathway**
- Notes: "Synapse first at the base of the Midbrain/top of Medulla … Primary Auditory Nucleus (A1)"
- Fixed: The **cochlear nuclei are in the medulla**. Then superior olive (pons) → inferior colliculus (midbrain) → MGN (thalamus) → **primary auditory cortex** (A1).
- Why: Location; A1 is cortex, not a nucleus.

**C-033 · Vestibular › Canals vs otoliths**
- Notes: "The vestibular system also starts in the air … Within each semicircular canal … Has a cap of calcium crystals"
- Fixed:
  - **Semicircular canals** (3, at right angles to each other) detect head **rotation**. Their hair cells sit in a gel **cupula with no crystals**.
  - The **otolith organs** (utricle and saccule) have the calcium carbonate crystals (otoconia). They detect **tilt and linear acceleration**.
  - The system senses head movement and gravity, not air.
- Why: The crystals are in a different organ.

**C-034 · Somatosensory › Dermatomes**
- Notes: "The spinal cord is organized into Dermatomes, different zones of the cord"
- Fixed: A dermatome is the **area of skin** served by one spinal nerve (one segment). Segments run cervical → thoracic → lumbar → sacral.
- Why: Dermatomes are on the skin, not in the cord.

**C-035 · Somatosensory › Nociceptors**
- Notes: "They respond to chemical substances released by damaged cells. Not responding to contact itself"
- Fixed: Many nociceptors are **polymodal**. They respond to **intense mechanical** force, **extreme temperature**, and **chemicals** released by damaged tissue.
- Why: Too narrow.

**C-036 · Eye › Conjunctiva**
- Notes: "Contacts the Conjunctiva, or eyelids"
- Fixed: The conjunctiva is the thin membrane that **lines the inside of the eyelids and folds back over the white of the eye**.
- Why: It is not the eyelids.

**C-037 · Eye › Sclera**
- Notes: "Behind the Iris is the Sclera"
- Fixed: The sclera is the tough **white outer wall** of the eyeball, continuous with the cornea at the front. The **lens** is what sits behind the iris.
- Why: Position.

**C-038 · Eye › Extraocular muscles**
- Notes: "Four exist, one top and bottom, and two medial-ish"
- Fixed: **Six**: four rectus muscles (superior, inferior, medial, lateral) and two obliques (superior, inferior).
- Why: Count.

**C-039 · Eye › Fovea**
- Notes: "Fovea is the most light-sensitive spot on the retina"
- Fixed: The fovea has the **highest acuity** (sharpest detail), packed with cones. **Rods** outside the fovea are more light-sensitive. That's why dim stars are easier to see out of the corner of your eye.
- Why: Acuity is not sensitivity.

**C-040 · Eye › Retinal neurons**
- Notes: "Ganglion Cells … Technically the only neurons in the chain"
- Fixed: Photoreceptors and bipolar cells **are neurons**. They use **graded potentials** instead of spikes. Ganglion cells are the only cells in the chain that fire **action potentials**.
- Why: Neuron ≠ spiking cell.

**C-041 · Eye › Cone distribution**
- Notes: "Basically no cones away from the Fovea"
- Fixed: Cones are found **across the whole retina**, just at **much lower density** outside the fovea. That's why you still see color in the periphery.
- Why: Overstated.

**C-042 · Central visual › Why decussation is partial**
- Notes: "Information processed on the edge of the visual field where there's no overlap will cross over"
- Fixed: Axons from the **nasal (inner) half of each retina** cross at the chiasm. Temporal-half axons don't cross. The nasal retina sees the temporal (outer) visual field, so the result is that **each hemisphere gets the opposite visual hemifield from both eyes**.
- Why: Crossing depends on which half of the retina, not on binocular overlap. (Your bitemporal hemianopia description is correct.)

**C-043 · Central visual › Retinotectal projection**
- Notes: "info sent to the Superior Colliculus … then to the Pulvinar … then to V1 … controlling pupil size … Micro Cicadas"
- Fixed:
  - The superior colliculus **orients the eyes and head** toward stimuli (saccades). Its route via the pulvinar goes mainly to **extrastriate** visual cortex.
  - Pupil size is controlled by a separate retinal projection to the **pretectum**.
  - "Micro cicadas" → **microsaccades**.
- Why: Targets and functions.

**C-044 · Central visual › Simple cells**
- Notes: "Several Ganglion Cells can feed into one Simple Cell"
- Fixed: Several **LGN** cells with aligned receptive fields feed a V1 simple cell. Ganglion cells reach V1 only by way of the LGN.
- Why: Missing relay.

**C-045 · Spinal control › Smooth muscle examples**
- Notes: "Smooth muscles … (heart, respiratory system, Iris, etc…)"
- Fixed: Smooth muscle lines the gut, blood vessels, airways and iris. The **heart is cardiac muscle** (striated but involuntary). Breathing is driven mainly by the **diaphragm**, a skeletal muscle.
- Why: The notes contradict themselves a few lines later.

**C-046 · Spinal control › Motor neuron pool**
- Notes: "All muscle fibers in an area for a specific muscle is a Motor Neuron Pool"
- Fixed: A **motor unit** is one alpha motor neuron plus all the fibers it innervates. A **motor neuron pool** is all the alpha motor neurons that innervate one muscle.
- Why: The first definition described fibers, not neurons (your second sentence was right).

**C-047 · Spinal control › Interneurons**
- Notes: "Spinal Interneurons (inhibitory signals …)"
- Fixed: Spinal interneurons are the **largest** input to alpha motor neurons and can be **excitatory or inhibitory**. Reciprocal inhibition uses the inhibitory ones.
- Why: Not only inhibitory.

**C-048 · Spinal control › ALS**
- Notes: "ALS … attacks LMNs"
- Fixed: ALS destroys **both upper and lower motor neurons**. The UMN + LMN combination is what defines it.
- Why: Incomplete.

**C-049 · Spinal control › Bell's palsy**
- Notes: "compression of cranial nerves (motor nerve 8)"
- Fixed: Dysfunction of the **facial nerve (cranial nerve VII)**, usually from inflammation (often viral). Cranial nerve VIII is the auditory-vestibular nerve.
- Why: Wrong nerve.

**C-050 · Spinal control › Stretch reflex**
- Notes: "When tendons are tapped, quads are relaxed slightly → muscle spindle detects …"
- Fixed: The tap **stretches** the quadriceps. Spindle Ia axons fire and excite the quadriceps alpha motor neurons monosynaptically, so the muscle contracts and the leg kicks.
- Why: Stretched, not relaxed.

**C-051 · Brain control › Motor hierarchy**
- Notes: "Lowest is Function"
- Fixed: The three levels are **Strategy** (association cortex, basal ganglia), **Tactics** (motor cortex, cerebellum) and **Execution** (brainstem, spinal cord).
- Why: Standard terms.

**C-052 · Brain control › Corticospinal origin**
- Notes: "Starts in PMC (anterior to central sulcus)"
- Fixed: Starts mostly in **M1 (area 4) and area 6** (premotor and supplementary motor areas), with some from somatosensory cortex. It crosses at the **pyramidal decussation** in the medulla.
- Why: "PMC" is ambiguous. M1 is the main source.

**C-053 · Brain control › Premotor area**
- Notes: "Contralateral organization (brain right side controls body left side) is done in the PMA"
- Fixed: Contralateral control comes from the **corticospinal tract crossing** in the medulla. The **PMA plans** movements: its neurons fire after an instruction cue and before the movement.
- Why: Two separate ideas were merged.

**C-054 · Brain control › Parkinson's**
- Notes: "shaking with attempted movement"
- Fixed: The classic Parkinson's tremor is a **resting tremor** that lessens with movement. The core problem is **difficulty initiating movement** (bradykinesia).
- Why: Tremor during movement is the cerebellar pattern.

**C-055 · Brain control › Hemiballismus**
- Notes: "Damage to Globus Pallidus can cause this"
- Fixed: Classically caused by damage to the **subthalamic nucleus**, on the side opposite the flailing limbs.
- Why: Wrong structure.

**C-056 · Brain control › Dysmetria**
- Notes: "Dyssmetria damages coordination, reduced awareness of limbs in space"
- Fixed: **Dysmetria** means movements that **overshoot or undershoot** their target (e.g., missing your nose on a finger-to-nose test). Limb-position *sense* is intact.
- Why: Definition.

### Exam 3: Higher Functions

**C-057 · Emotion › Cannon-Bard**
- Notes: "your brain processes emotional response, then sends signals producing an autonomic response"
- Fixed: The thalamus sends signals **simultaneously** to the cortex (the felt emotion) and to the body (the autonomic response). Neither causes the other.
- Why: Simultaneity is Cannon-Bard's key claim.

**C-058 · Emotion › Epinephrine ("vitamin") studies**
- Notes: listed as "more evidence for James-Lange"
- Fixed: Schachter & Singer (1962) is the classic support for **two-factor theory**: emotion = physiological arousal + a cognitive label from context. It agrees with James-Lange that the body matters, but shows that the label decides *which* emotion you feel. (Epinephrine was given as a hormone injection, not as a neurotransmitter.)
- Why: Standard interpretation.

**C-059 · Emotion › Sham rage**
- Notes: "Chopping off most of the front brain (cerebral cortex + hypothalamus) leaves an animal functional. Leaving the hypothalamus will cause Sham Rage"
- Fixed: Removing the **cerebral cortex but leaving the hypothalamus** produces sham rage. If the posterior hypothalamus is also removed, the rage response disappears.
- Why: The first sentence says the hypothalamus was removed.

**C-060 · Emotion › Hypothalamus and facial expression**
- Notes: "Hypothalamus has a direct connection to the muscles"
- Fixed: The hypothalamus drives involuntary expressions **through brainstem motor nuclei**, which control the facial muscles. It doesn't connect to the muscles directly.
- Why: No direct pathway.

**C-061 · Emotion › Klüver-Bucy**
- Notes: "Amygdala, when removed … known as Kluver-Bucy Syndrome … seen in humans (due to mostly accidental Amygdala removal)"
- Fixed: Klüver and Bucy removed **both temporal lobes** (including the amygdala) in monkeys. The amygdala accounts for the loss of fear. In humans the syndrome follows **bilateral temporal lobe damage**: surgery, trauma, or herpes encephalitis.
- Why: The extent of the lesion.

**C-062 · Emotion › Fear conditioning vs. the Garcia effect**
- Notes: "When neutral responses are paired with dangerous stimuli (Garcia effect), fear responses will increase"
- Fixed: This is **Pavlovian fear conditioning**. The **Garcia effect** is **conditioned taste aversion**: a taste paired with later sickness. (Your Chemical Senses notes describe it correctly.)
- Why: Two different phenomena.

**C-063 · Emotion › Area 25**
- Notes: "Deep brain stimulation to an area in the amygdala shown to alleviate depression — Area 25"
- Fixed: Area 25 is the **subgenual cingulate cortex** (below the front of the corpus callosum), not the amygdala. Mayberg's DBS studies targeted it in treatment-resistant depression.
- Why: Location.

**C-064 · Attention › Covert attention**
- Notes: "Covert Attention is the ability to focus on something directly as well as be aware of other visual areas"
- Fixed: Covert attention means attending to a location **without moving your eyes to it**. In Posner's cueing task, valid cues speed detection, neutral cues are slower, and invalid cues are slowest.
- Why: The definition said the opposite of "without looking". The rest of your notes were right.

**C-065 · Attention › V4**
- Notes: "V4 directly, which cares about luminance and such"
- Fixed: V4 is tuned to **color and form**. (Your earlier line, "Color is processed in V4", is right.)
- Why: Internal inconsistency.

**C-066 · Attention › Hemispatial neglect**
- Notes: "completely gets rid of awareness of one half of the visual field … More likely to happen on the LEFT hemisphere"
- Fixed: Damage to the **right** posterior parietal cortex causes neglect of the **left side of space**: the left side of the visual field, of objects, and even of the body. It is an **attention** problem, not blindness; the visual pathway works. The right hemisphere attends to both sides, while the left mostly attends to the right, so left-side damage is compensated.
- Why: "Left hemisphere" should be "left side of space". It's attention, not vision.

**C-067 · Language › Conduction aphasia**
- Notes: "The connection between A1 and M1 is being disrupted"
- Fixed: Damage to the **arcuate fasciculus** disconnects **Wernicke's area from Broca's area**.
- Why: Your next bullet says this correctly.

**C-068 · Language › Split brain**
- Notes: "The right side can process a word because language and other necessary systems are both on the left. Can't say a word on the left, but can draw a picture"
- Fixed: A word shown to the **left visual field** reaches only the **right hemisphere**. The person **can't say it**, because language is in the left hemisphere. The right hemisphere still understands enough to **draw it or pick the object with the left hand**.
- Why: The sentence was garbled.

**C-069 · Memory › Semantic memory**
- Notes: "Somatic memory is something like an actual fact"
- Fixed: **Semantic** memory = facts and general knowledge. Episodic memory = personal events.
- Why: Term.

**C-070 · Memory › Baddeley's model**
- Notes: "it moves to the Episodic Buffer (equivalent of short-term memory) and finally the Long Term Memory"
- Fixed: Working memory has a **central executive** that directs three subsystems: the **phonological loop**, the **visuospatial sketchpad**, and the **episodic buffer**. All three exchange information with long-term memory. The episodic buffer **combines** information from the others and from LTM. It isn't a separate later stage.
- Why: Model structure.

**C-071 · Memory › Phineas Gage**
- Notes: "Phineas Gage who had deficits to working memory"
- Fixed: Gage's documented changes were in **personality, social behavior and decision-making**. His memory was never tested. The evidence that the PFC is needed for working memory comes from the **Wisconsin Card Sort**, **delayed-response studies in monkeys**, and imaging.
- Why: Not supported by the case record.

**C-072 · Memory › Delayed non-match-to-sample**
- Notes: "Shows the medial temporal structures (MTL) are critical for moving info into short term memory … Structures just outside the hippocampus are more important for short term memory"
- Fixed: The task shows that the MTL (especially the **perirhinal, entorhinal and parahippocampal cortices**) is critical for **recognition memory over a delay**, which is declarative memory. Short-term retention is spared at short delays, which is why performance only drops as the delay grows.
- Why: The deficit grows with delay, which points to memory formation, not short-term memory.

**C-073 · Memory › AMPA/NMDA**
- Notes: "When glutamate enters the cell it causes depolarization"
- Fixed: Glutamate **binds** AMPA receptors (it doesn't enter the cell). Na⁺ flows in, the cell depolarizes, and that pushes the Mg²⁺ block out of NMDA receptors.
- Why: Mechanism.

**C-074 · Memory › Consolidation and proteins**
- Notes: "When you want things to last a really long time you have changes without proteins through Kinase and protein synthesis"
- Fixed: Phosphorylating existing proteins (by kinases) strengthens synapses for **hours**. Long-lasting memory needs **new protein synthesis** (triggered through CREB) and structural change at the synapse.
- Why: The sentence was self-contradictory.

**C-075 · Memory › Protein synthesis inhibitors**
- Notes: "Injecting rats with protein inhibitors causes no long term learning"
- Fixed: **Protein synthesis inhibitors** block **long-term** memory consolidation. Short-term memory is left intact.
- Why: Precision.

**C-076 · Memory › CREB**
- Notes: "adding CREB gives you super memory"
- Fixed: Boosting CREB in animal studies (flies, mice) made **long-term memory form faster**, with fewer training trials. Blocking CREB prevents it.
- Why: Overstated.

**C-077 · Mental illness › HPA axis**
- Notes: "Adrenal Glands (in kidneys)"
- Fixed: Adrenal glands sit **on top of** the kidneys. The chain is hypothalamus (CRH) → pituitary (ACTH, into the blood) → adrenal cortex (cortisol).
- Why: Anatomy.

**C-078 · Mental illness › Agoraphobia**
- Notes: "fear of being in a public situation with a lot of people … triggered by being around other people"
- Fixed: Fear of situations where **escape might be difficult or help unavailable** if panic strikes. Examples: public transport, open or enclosed spaces, crowds, being outside alone. The fear is about being trapped, not other people's judgment; that's social anxiety.
- Why: Definition.

**C-079 · Mental illness › Benzodiazepines**
- Notes: "all these involve GABA agonists"
- Fixed: Benzodiazepines are **positive allosteric modulators** of GABA_A receptors. They bind a separate site and make GABA open the Cl⁻ channel more effectively. They don't open the channel on their own. Alcohol also enhances GABA_A.
- Why: Mechanism (the effect, more inhibition, is the same).

**C-080 · Mental illness › SSRIs**
- Notes: "SSRIs (Serotonin Selective Reuptake Inhibitors). These inhibit Serotonin reuptake receptors"
- Fixed: **Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors**. They block the serotonin **transporter (SERT)**.
- Why: Name; a transporter is not a receptor.

**C-081 · Mental illness › COMT and cannabis**
- Notes: "COMT helps code for proteins and NT deactivation … Met/Met or Met/Val have low risk, but Val/Val have much higher chances … IF they take marijuana in adolescence"
- Fixed: COMT encodes an **enzyme that breaks down dopamine** (and other catecholamines). In Caspi et al. (2005), adolescent cannabis use raised psychosis risk most in **Val/Val** carriers, somewhat in **Val/Met**, and not in **Met/Met**.
- Why: Val/Met carriers did show increased risk.

**C-082 · Mental illness › Glutamate hypothesis**
- Notes: "Less Glutamate leads to psychotic symptoms"
- Fixed: The hypothesis is **reduced NMDA-receptor function** (NMDA hypofunction). PCP and ketamine block NMDA receptors and mimic positive and negative symptoms.
- Why: The receptor hypofunction is the key point, not the amount of glutamate.
