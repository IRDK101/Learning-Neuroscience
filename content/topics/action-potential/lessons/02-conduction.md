## One way down the axon {#direction}

::diagram{id="neuron" highlight="axon-hillock,axon" caption="The spike starts at the hillock and travels toward the terminals."}

An action potential starts at one point and travels down the axon like a wave of opening gates. The normal direction, from the cell body toward the terminals, is **orthodromic**. The reverse, **antidromic**, can be forced in the lab but doesn't happen naturally. The spike can't run backward because the Na⁺ channels just behind it are still inactivated.

## Seen from different places {#recording}

The textbook "spike" is recorded from *inside* the cell. Recorded from *outside*, the same event looks roughly upside down. The shape differs a little depending on where you record, but it's the same electrical exchange seen from a different place.

## Speed {#speed}

::focus{highlight="myelin-sheath,node-of-ranvier"}

A typical action potential travels around **10 m/s**, and two things change that:

- **Axon diameter:** a wider axon has less resistance, so it conducts faster.
- **Myelin:** insulation keeps current from leaking out, so the signal travels faster. The spike regenerates only at the **nodes of Ranvier** and effectively jumps from node to node. This is **saltatory conduction**.

::check{q="q-ap-myelin"}

## Coding strength {#frequency}

Because every spike is the same size, a stronger message is sent as **more spikes per second**, not bigger ones. More current means a higher firing rate, up to the limit set by the refractory period.
