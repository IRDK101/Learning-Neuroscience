## The amygdala's parts {#nuclei}

::diagram{id="limbic-system" highlight="amygdala" caption="The amygdala sits just in front of the hippocampus."}

The [[amygdala]] lies deep in the medial temporal lobe, just in front of the hippocampus, one in each hemisphere. It has several groups of nuclei:

- The **[[basolateral nuclei|basolateral-amygdala]]** receive input from the senses and send some output to the cortex.
- The **[[corticomedial nuclei|corticomedial-amygdala]]**, at the top, receive smell.
- The **[[central nucleus|central-amygdala]]** is the output, reaching the hypothalamus, brainstem, thalamus and cortex.

## Life without an amygdala {#lesions}

When Klüver and Bucy removed both temporal lobes in monkeys, including the amygdalae, the animals lost their fear and became tame. They also put objects in their mouths, ate more, became hypersexual and had trouble recognizing things by sight. This is [[Klüver-Bucy syndrome|kluver-bucy-syndrome]], and it also follows damage to both temporal lobes in people. The loss of fear comes from the amygdala: without it, a threat no longer sets off a fast, automatic reaction.

In the rare genetic [[Urbach-Wiethe disease|urbach-wiethe-disease]], calcium deposits destroy both amygdalae. Patients struggle to recognize fear in faces, because they don't naturally look at the eyes. Told to look at the eyes, they do much better. Wide eyes showing lots of white are a strong fear signal; even flashing just the eyes of a fearful face activates the amygdala. Other people's fear is a good clue that something nearby is dangerous.

## Learning fear {#fear-learning}

The amygdala also learns what to fear. In [[fear conditioning|fear-conditioning]], a neutral cue (a tone) is paired with something unpleasant (a shock). The basolateral nuclei link the two, so afterwards the tone alone makes the central nucleus drive the hypothalamus and brainstem into a fear response. This usually takes several pairings, but one overwhelming experience can be enough, as in PTSD.

The amygdala doesn't work alone. Prefrontal areas, including the [[dorsolateral prefrontal cortex|dorsolateral-prefrontal-cortex]], the anterior cingulate and the orbitofrontal cortex, supply context: a snake in the wild is frightening, the same snake behind glass at the zoo is not.

## Aggression {#aggression}

**Predatory** aggression is hunting. **Affective** aggression is a display aimed at others of the same species, to scare them off or assert rank ([[aggression]]). Damaging the amygdala reduces affective aggression, and the animal falls in the social hierarchy. The hypothalamus is heavily involved as well.

## The hypothalamus and rage {#hypothalamus}

::diagram{id="brain-midsagittal" highlight="hypothalamus" caption="The hypothalamus sits below the thalamus."}

When the cerebral cortex was removed from cats but the hypothalamus left intact, the cats flew into rages at harmless things: [[sham rage|sham-rage]]. They arched their backs, bared claws and snarled, and their blood pressure rose, pupils widened and fur stood up. Also removing the back of the hypothalamus abolished the rage, and stimulating the hypothalamus produces rage aimed at whatever is nearby. The hypothalamus can produce the whole display by itself; the cortex normally keeps it in check and in context.

The hypothalamus also drives **involuntary** expressions. A genuine smile or laugh is triggered through the hypothalamus and limbic system, which act through brainstem motor nuclei, not through the voluntary motor cortex. That's why a posed smile looks subtly different from a real one ([[involuntary expressions|involuntary-expression]]).
