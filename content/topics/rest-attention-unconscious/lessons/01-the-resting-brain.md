## Three states {#states}

Roughly speaking, the brain can be **resting**, **attending** or **unconscious** ([[brain states|brain-states]]). The boundaries are blurry, and each state just shifts which regions are more or less active.

- **Resting** here doesn't mean thinking about nothing. It means not focusing on anything in the world around you.
- **Attending** means focusing on something outside you.
- **Unconscious** covers sleep, anesthesia, coma and other unresponsive states.

## The default mode network {#dmn}

::diagram{id="default-mode-network" highlight="medial-prefrontal-cortex,posterior-cingulate-cortex" caption="The two midline hubs; the third is on the side of the brain."}

Researchers scanned people doing simple tasks separated by rest breaks. A set of regions kept becoming **more** active during the breaks and **less** active during any task. These regions form the [[default mode network|default-mode-network]]. Its main hubs are the [[posterior cingulate cortex|posterior-cingulate-cortex]], the [[medial prefrontal cortex|medial-prefrontal-cortex]] and the [[temporoparietal junction|temporoparietal-junction]]. Although they're far apart, their activity rises and falls together.

::check{q="q-rest-dmn"}

## What is it for? {#function}

An early idea, the [[sentinel hypothesis|sentinel-hypothesis]], was that the network keeps a loose watch on the surroundings like a lookout. Few researchers hold it now.

The favored idea is the [[internal mentation hypothesis|internal-mentation-hypothesis]]: the network supports **thinking about things that aren't in front of you**, such as remembering the past or imagining the future. Prompts to think about past or future events activate default mode regions more than control prompts do, as if they support "mental time travel".

::check{q="q-rest-mentation"}

## When it goes wrong {#illness}

The default mode network is disrupted in almost every mental illness. It's **overactive in depression**, which may make it a network for rumination, going over the same thoughts again and again. Psychedelic drugs turn it down and shift attention outward.
