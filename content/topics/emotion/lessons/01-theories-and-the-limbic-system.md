## Body first, or brain first? {#theories}

You see a bear, your heart pounds, and you feel afraid. But which causes which?

- The **[[James-Lange theory|james-lange-theory]]** says the body comes first. The autonomic nervous system reacts, and the feeling is the cortex's reading of those body changes ("bottom-up"). It can't be the whole story: you can feel sad without crying.
- The **[[Cannon-Bard theory|cannon-bard-theory]]** says the thalamus sends signals to the cortex (the feeling) and to the body (the response) **at the same time**, so neither causes the other.

Elements of both are probably true. Some evidence favors the body's role: people with spinal cord injuries that cut off feedback from the body have been reported to feel emotions less intensely.

## Arousal plus a label {#two-factor}

In the classic [[epinephrine experiments|schachter-singer-experiment]], people were injected with epinephrine (adrenaline), told it was a vitamin, then left to wait with an actor who behaved angrily. They became angrier than people given saline. But people who had been told to expect a racing heart and shaky hands didn't get angrier: they already had an explanation. With a cheerful actor, the epinephrine group felt happier instead.

This supports the **[[two-factor theory|two-factor-theory]]**: the body supplies arousal, and the situation supplies the label. The [[bridge experiment|bridge-experiment]] fits too: men who had just crossed a scary, swaying bridge were more likely to call an attractive interviewer afterwards, perhaps misreading fear as attraction.

## Basic emotions {#basic}

Five or six [[basic emotions|basic-emotions]] (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust and perhaps surprise) come with facial expressions that appear in every culture, even isolated ones, and similar faces appear in other animals.

No single region handles each emotion. Each one activates a pattern of many overlapping regions, differing in which areas join in and how strongly. Some links are causal, though: stimulating one part of the cingulate cortex can relieve sadness. The [[circumplex model|circumplex-model]] simplifies emotions onto two axes, arousal and valence (pleasant to unpleasant), but it can't capture mixed feelings.

## The limbic system {#limbic}

::diagram{id="limbic-system" highlight="cingulate-cortex,hippocampus,amygdala" caption="Arrows trace the Papez circuit."}

Broca described a ring of cortex around the brain's core on its inner surface, the **[[limbic lobe|limbic-lobe]]**. Papez later proposed that these structures form a loop for emotion, the [[Papez circuit|papez-circuit]]:

::pathway{id="papez-circuit"}

::focus{highlight="fornix,mammillary-bodies,thalamus,cingulate-cortex"}

The hippocampus sends output through the [[fornix]] to the [[mammillary bodies|mammillary-bodies]] of the hypothalamus, then to the anterior thalamus, the cingulate cortex, and back. The idea: deep structures like the hypothalamus produce and sense the body's responses, the cingulate cortex feels the basic emotion, and the surrounding neocortex adds context, such as *why* you're angry. Information loops around, which is why both top-down and bottom-up accounts of emotion can be partly right.

Today's [[limbic system|limbic-system]] includes the amygdala too. Its structures do more than emotion: the hippocampus, for instance, is mainly about memory.

::check{q="q-emo-papez"}
