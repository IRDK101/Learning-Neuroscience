## What is a mental illness? {#definition}

A [[mental illness|mental-illness-definition]] changes emotion, thinking or behavior enough to make everyday life hard. Almost all are diagnosed from **symptoms**, not biological tests; no brain scan can diagnose depression today. That doesn't make them any less biological, only harder to measure. Diagnoses are defined in the [[DSM|dsm]], which lists symptom-based criteria. Three major groups are anxiety disorders, mood disorders and schizophrenia.

## Anxiety disorders {#anxiety}

[[Anxiety disorders|anxiety-disorders]] involve tension and worry plus strong **body** responses from the autonomic nervous system: sweating, trembling, a racing heart, raised blood pressure, wide pupils, an upset stomach. They become clinically significant when panic is acute or worry is chronic.

Brain and body feed each other. Worry causes body changes, which cause more worry. Poor sleep and concentration make the problem harder to deal with, so it snowballs. Typical thinking patterns include **black-and-white thinking** (all good or all bad), **catastrophizing** (spiraling from a small thing to the worst outcome) and **mind-reading** (assuming others think badly of you).

- A **[[panic attack|panic-attack]]** is a short burst, usually under half an hour, of overwhelming fear with a pounding heart, trouble breathing and a sense of doom. It can come out of nowhere and be mistaken for a heart attack.
- **[[Agoraphobia]]** is fear of situations where escape might be hard or help unavailable if panic strikes, such as crowds, public transport or open spaces. In severe cases people stop leaving home.
- **[[Obsessive-compulsive disorder|ocd]]** combines **obsessions** (intrusive, distressing thoughts) with **compulsions** (repeated actions to relieve them), with themes like contamination, ordering or hoarding.

## The HPA stress axis {#hpa}

::diagram{id="hpa-axis" highlight="hypothalamus,pituitary-gland,adrenal-gland" caption="The amygdala drives the loop; the hippocampus brakes it."}

::pathway{id="hpa-axis"}

The [[HPA axis|hpa-axis]] is the body's stress-hormone chain. The **hypothalamus** releases CRH, the **pituitary** releases ACTH into the blood, and the [[adrenal glands|adrenal-gland]], on top of the kidneys, release [[cortisol]]. Cortisol acts all over the body, supporting fight or flight. Some is useful in a crisis; too much for too long is harmful, and a dysregulated HPA axis may contribute to anxiety.

::focus{highlight="amygdala,hippocampus"}

Two regions pull the axis in opposite directions:

- The **amygdala** pushes it up, so more cortisol is released.
- The **hippocampus** brakes it. It has [[glucocorticoid receptors|glucocorticoid-receptor]] that sense cortisol and shut the axis down when there's too much.

People with anxiety tend to have **fewer** glucocorticoid receptors in the hippocampus, so the brake is weaker and the system more easily runs out of control.

## Treating anxiety {#treatment}

**Psychotherapy** works well. In [[cognitive behavioral therapy|cbt]], people learn strategies to break the loop between thoughts, feelings and behavior. Challenging anxious thoughts works top-down. Calming the body with slow breathing or muscle relaxation works bottom-up, in the spirit of the James-Lange theory. Changing behavior changes the brain, even without a drug.

**Drugs** help too:

- **[[Benzodiazepines|benzodiazepines]]** (such as Xanax) are [[anxiolytics|anxiolytic]]. They bind a separate site on GABA_A receptors and make GABA open the Cl⁻ channel more effectively, increasing inhibition. Alcohol also enhances GABA_A receptors, which may be why alcohol misuse and anxiety often go together. People with anxiety disorders may have fewer GABA receptors to begin with.
- **[[SSRIs|ssris]]** (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) block the serotonin transporter, so serotonin stays in the synapse longer. They take two to three weeks to work, although reuptake is blocked within hours. The benefit probably comes from slower changes downstream, such as more glucocorticoid receptors.
