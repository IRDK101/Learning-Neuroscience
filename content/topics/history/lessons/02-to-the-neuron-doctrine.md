## Mind and machine {#descartes}

In the 17th century, **René Descartes** {{C-003}} argued for *dualism*: the mind is completely separate from the body. He placed their meeting point in the tiny **pineal gland**. (It actually secretes melatonin, which regulates sleep.)

As preservation techniques improved, anatomists could look at the brain itself. They told **grey matter**, the outer covering where cell bodies sit, apart from **white matter**, the connections underneath. They also noticed that the folds are not random: the bumps (gyri) and grooves (sulci) are consistent across people. Folding packs a huge surface into a small skull.

## Electricity {#galvani}

**Luigi Galvani** (1737–1798) made dissected frog legs twitch by connecting them to electricity, even to a lightning rod during storms. Nerves, he showed, work electrically.

## The phrenology mistake {#phrenology}

**Franz Gall** (1758–1828) went too far. His *phrenology* (1796) claimed that bumps on the skull reveal the size of the brain areas underneath, and therefore your personality. It has no basis in reality and fed bigoted pseudoscience. It does contain one real idea: different brain regions do different jobs.

## Patients who taught us about language {#broca-wernicke}

::diagram{id="brain-lateral" highlight="brocas-area,wernickes-area" caption="Broca's and Wernicke's areas, usually in the left hemisphere."}

::focus{highlight="brocas-area"}

**Paul Broca** (1824–1880) got to know patients who had trouble speaking after strokes, then examined their brains after death. They had damage in the same spot in the left frontal lobe, now **Broca's area**. In **Broca's aphasia**, turning thoughts into words breaks down, though patients can often still sing words. It affects sign language too.

::focus{highlight="wernickes-area"}

**Carl Wernicke** (1848–1905) described the opposite problem. Patients with damage further back, in **Wernicke's area**, speak fluently but understand little, so their speech becomes "word salad". Wernicke also predicted *disconnection disorders*: damage to the wiring between two areas can cause problems even when both areas are fine.

## Phineas Gage {#gage}

::focus{highlight="prefrontal-cortex"}

**Phineas Gage** was a railroad worker who survived an iron rod blasted through his frontal lobe. He went from mild-mannered to rude, aggressive and impulsive. His case linked the frontal lobes to emotion, decision-making and personality.

## Seeing cells {#stains}

- **Theodor Schwann** helped develop **cell theory** (1839): all tissue is made of cells.
- **Korbinian Brodmann** (1868–1918) {{C-004}} mapped the cortex into 52 areas by their cell types and layering. Brodmann areas are still used.
- **Franz Nissl**'s stain colors the cell bodies of *all* cells, which is good for counting and mapping them.
- **Camillo Golgi**'s stain colors only a few random neurons, but colors each one *completely*, branches and all {{C-005}}. Golgi himself wrongly thought the brain was one continuous net.
- **Santiago Ramón y Cajal** (1852–1934) used Golgi's stain to show the opposite: the brain is made of many separate neurons that form circuits. This is the **neuron doctrine**, the foundation of everything that follows.
