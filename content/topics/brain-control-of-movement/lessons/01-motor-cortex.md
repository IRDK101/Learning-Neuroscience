## Three levels {#hierarchy}

Before lower motor neurons can act, the brain has to decide what to do. Control works on three levels ([[motor hierarchy|motor-hierarchy]]), and all three are needed for any action:

1. **Strategy**: what's the goal? Thirsty, so get water. Association cortex and the basal ganglia, drawing on memory and plans rather than any one sense.
2. **Tactics**: which movements will reach it? Reach for the bottle. Motor cortex and cerebellum.
3. **Execution**: carry them out. Grab the bottle. Brainstem and spinal cord.

## Descending tracts {#tracts}

Motor tracts run down the ventral half of the cord in two groups:

- [[Lateral pathways|lateral-pathways]], further out, control **voluntary** limb movements. They include the corticospinal and rubrospinal tracts.
- [[Ventromedial pathways|ventromedial-pathways]], near the middle, handle **automatic** control: posture, balance and small adjustments.

## The corticospinal tract {#corticospinal}

::diagram{id="long-tracts" highlight="primary-motor-cortex" layers="motor" caption="The motor pathway for the right side of the body."}

::pathway{id="corticospinal-tract"}

[[Upper motor neurons|upper-motor-neuron]] in M1 and area 6 send a single long axon all the way down. It passes through the brainstem, **crosses at the bottom of the medulla** (the pyramidal decussation), and runs down the opposite side of the cord in the white matter beside the ventral horn. There it synapses on lower motor neurons, directly or through interneurons. These are some of the longest cells in the body. Because the tract crosses, each hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body.

::check{q="q-move-cross"}

## M1 {#m1}

::diagram{id="brain-lateral" highlight="primary-motor-cortex" caption="M1 is the strip just in front of the central sulcus."}

The [[primary motor cortex|primary-motor-cortex]] (M1, area 4) lies on the precentral gyrus, just in front of the central sulcus. Like S1 behind it, it's a body map. The amount of cortex for each part follows how much **fine control** it needs, not its size: lots for the hands and the mouth (speech needs very precise coordination), little for the legs.

An upper motor neuron may excite an extensor's motor neurons directly while, through an inhibitory interneuron, silencing the matching flexor, or any combination of the two.

## How M1 codes a movement {#coding}

M1 neurons fire most **just before** a movement starts, and the more force the brain predicts it will need, the faster they fire.

They also code **direction**. Each neuron has a preferred direction and fires less the further the movement is from it. No neuron exists for every possible angle. Instead, the movement goes in the direction of the combined "vote" of many neurons, each weighted by how fast it fires, like adding vectors ([[population coding|population-coding]]).

## Planning areas {#planning}

In front of M1 is area 6, made up of the [[premotor area|premotor-area]] (PMA) on the side and the [[supplementary motor area|supplementary-motor-area]] (SMA) toward the top. The PMA **plans** movements: its neurons fire after an instruction ("get ready to reach left") and before the movement, deciding what to do before the upper motor neurons carry it out. They quiet down once the movement is done.

Some premotor neurons, [[mirror neurons|mirror-neurons]], fire both when a monkey does an action and when it watches someone else do it. They're clearly shown in monkeys; whether humans have them in the same form is debated. They've been proposed as a basis for understanding others' intentions, and perhaps empathy.
