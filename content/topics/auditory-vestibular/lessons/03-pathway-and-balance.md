## Up to the cortex {#pathway}

::pathway{id="auditory-pathway"}

Spiral ganglion axons enter the brainstem and synapse in the [[cochlear nuclei|cochlear-nuclei]] of the medulla. From there signals go to the [[superior olive|superior-olive]] on **both** sides, then to the [[inferior colliculus|inferior-colliculus]] in the midbrain, the [[medial geniculate nucleus|medial-geniculate-nucleus]] (MGN) of the thalamus, and finally the [[primary auditory cortex|primary-auditory-cortex]] (A1), just below the lateral fissure.

::check{q="q-aud-path"}

## Where is that sound? {#localize}

The superior olive is the first place where input from the two ears meets. A sound on your left reaches your left ear a fraction of a millisecond before your right ear, and it's a little louder there. Neurons in the superior olive detect those differences, which tells the brain where the sound came from.

## Coding pitch and loudness {#coding}

Each spiral ganglion cell has a favorite frequency and fires most to sounds near it. Louder sounds make it fire more. A1 keeps the [[tonotopic|tonotopy]] map: its front (anterior) end responds to low pitches and its back end to high pitches.

::check{q="q-aud-a1"}

## Balance {#vestibular}

::diagram{id="ear" highlight="semicircular-canals,otolith-organs" caption="The vestibular organs sit beside the cochlea."}

The [[vestibular labyrinth|vestibular-labyrinth]] senses how the head moves and which way gravity pulls. It uses hair cells, just like the cochlea.

- The three [[semicircular canals|semicircular-canals]] sit at right angles to each other, one for each axis of rotation. When you turn your head, the fluid inside lags behind and bends hair cells sitting in a gel cap (the cupula).
- The two [[otolith organs|otolith-organs]] (utricle and saccule) have gel weighed down by tiny calcium carbonate crystals. When you tilt your head or speed up in a straight line, the crystals drag the gel and bend the hair cells.

::check{q="q-aud-otolith"}

The vestibular system works mostly without your noticing. The [[vestibulo-ocular reflex|vestibulo-ocular-reflex]] moves your eyes opposite to your head so your gaze stays fixed, which is how you can read a sign while walking. When your eyes and your vestibular system disagree, as when reading in a moving car, the mismatch can cause [[motion sickness|motion-sickness]].
