## A limited resource {#limits}

[[Attention|attention]] picks out a little of what's around you for priority processing. It's very limited: split it between two demanding tasks, like reading carefully with lyrics playing, and both suffer.

## Bottom-up and top-down {#two-kinds}

- **[[Bottom-up attention|bottom-up-attention]]** is grabbed by the world. Something that stands out, like a red stop sign in a green landscape or a sudden bang, pulls your attention whether you meant to look or not. In the [[cocktail party effect|cocktail-party-effect]], you're deep in one conversation but notice at once when someone across the room says your name.
- **[[Top-down attention|top-down-attention]]** is directed on purpose, by your goals. Asked to judge people's ages in a painting, you look at different parts of it than when asked about their clothes, and you'll remember different things.

::check{q="q-att-bottom-up"}

## Change blindness {#change-blindness}

We see changes mainly in what we're attending to. If your attention is pulled elsewhere, by a flicker or a distracting object, even a large change in a scene can go unnoticed ([[change blindness|change-blindness]]).

## Covert attention {#covert}

You can attend to a place **without moving your eyes to it**: [[covert attention|covert-attention]]. In the [[cueing task|posner-cueing-task]], people keep their eyes on a central point while a cue hints where a target will appear:

- A **valid** cue (pointing to the right side) gives the fastest, most accurate responses.
- A **neutral** cue is slower.
- An **invalid** cue (pointing the wrong way) is slowest.

::check{q="q-att-covert"}

## Attention prepares the cortex {#preparation}

Attention changes processing **before** anything appears ([[attention prepares the cortex|attention-preactivation]]):

- Cue a **location**, and the matching part of V1's map becomes more active ahead of time. Central vision maps to the back of V1, and the periphery further forward.
- Cue a **feature**, and the area specialized for it becomes more active: V4 for color, V5 for motion.

## Who steers attention? {#control}

::diagram{id="attention-areas" highlight="frontal-eye-fields,posterior-parietal-cortex" caption="Two cortical areas that direct attention."}

- The [[pulvinar]], at the back of the thalamus, sees visual information from the retina and V1 and helps decide which parts get priority, influencing V1 and the later visual areas.
- The [[frontal eye fields|frontal-eye-fields]], in front of the premotor cortex, control voluntary eye movements and hold a map of the visual field. Stimulating one spot boosts responses in the matching part of V4, which is tuned to color and form, like a spotlight. Stimulating the wrong spot pulls attention away.

::check{q="q-att-fef"}

## Hemispatial neglect {#neglect}

Damage to the **right** [[posterior parietal cortex|posterior-parietal-cortex]] can cause [[hemispatial neglect|hemispatial-neglect]]: people ignore the **left side of space**. They may eat only the food on the right of the plate, shave only the right side of the face, or copy only the right half of a drawing, and they don't realize anything is wrong. Their vision works; the problem is attention.

Why the right side? The right hemisphere attends to both sides of space, while the left attends mostly to the right. If the left hemisphere is damaged, the right can cover; if the right is damaged, the left side of space is left unattended. (Similar reasoning explains why left-hemisphere damage hits language.)

::check{q="q-att-neglect"}

## Seeing without knowing {#blindsight}

After damage to V1, some people say they see nothing in part of their visual field, yet they can point to or avoid objects there: [[blindsight]]. Routes that bypass V1, such as the one through the superior colliculus and pulvinar, can guide behavior without conscious seeing.
