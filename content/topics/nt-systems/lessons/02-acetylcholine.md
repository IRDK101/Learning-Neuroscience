## Making acetylcholine {#synthesis}

::pathway{id="acetylcholine-cycle"}

Acetylcholine (ACh) is built in the axon terminal from two pieces: **choline** and an **acetyl group** (from acetyl CoA). The enzyme [[choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)|choline-acetyltransferase]] joins them. A **vesicular transporter** then loads the ACh into vesicles, ready for release when an action potential arrives.

The **rate-limiting step** is the supply of **choline**. The body makes only a little, so most has to come from food {{C-021}}.

::check{q="q-nt-ach-rate"}

## Clearing acetylcholine {#deactivation}

::diagram{id="synapse" highlight="acetylcholinesterase" caption="In a cholinergic synapse, the enzyme in the cleft is AChE."}

Once ACh is in the cleft and has bound its receptors, it's destroyed very quickly by [[acetylcholinesterase (AChE)|acetylcholinesterase]], which splits it into choline and acetate.

::focus{highlight="reuptake-transporter"}

The **choline** is then carried back into the presynaptic terminal (together with sodium) by a transporter, to be made into ACh again. Note that it's the *choline* that is taken back up, not intact ACh.

::check{q="q-nt-ache"}

## Where acetylcholine comes from {#sources}

::diagram{id="nt-overlay" layers="ach,deep" highlight="nucleus-basalis,medial-septal-nuclei,pmt-complex"}

Cholinergic neurons in the brain sit in two places:

::focus{highlight="medial-septal-nuclei,nucleus-basalis" layers="ach,deep"}

- The **basal forebrain complex**: the **medial septal nuclei** and the **basal nucleus of Meynert**.

::focus{highlight="pmt-complex" layers="ach,deep"}

- The **pontomesencephalotegmental (PMT) complex**, high in the brainstem where the pons meets the midbrain.

There are only a few of these cells, but their axons spread very widely: around the cortex, the thalamus and the hippocampus, down the brainstem, and around the temporal lobe.


::check{q="q-nt-ach-sources"}

## What acetylcholine does {#functions}

- At the **neuromuscular junction**, ACh makes skeletal muscle contract. Every voluntary movement depends on it.
- Through the parasympathetic nervous system it **slows the heart** and acts on smooth muscle, helping regulate heart rate and blood pressure {{C-023}}.
- In the brain it **assists learning and memory**.

It's also the classic example of the receptor deciding the effect: the same molecule excites skeletal muscle and inhibits the heart.
