## The basal ganglia {#basal-ganglia}

::diagram{id="brain-coronal" highlight="caudate-nucleus,putamen,globus-pallidus" caption="The basal ganglia sit deep in each hemisphere."}

The [[basal ganglia|basal-ganglia]] are deep forebrain nuclei: the caudate nucleus and putamen (together, the [[striatum]]) and the globus pallidus. They work closely with the [[substantia nigra|substantia-nigra]] and the [[subthalamic nucleus|subthalamic-nucleus]].

::pathway{id="basal-ganglia-loop"}

Put simply, they sit in a loop with the frontal lobe. They get a copy of what the planning areas intend, compare it with what's happening, and send the result back through the thalamus to the motor cortex and SMA, refining the plan.

## Go and stop {#direct-indirect}

Two routes through the loop pull in opposite directions ([[direct and indirect pathways|direct-indirect-pathways]]):

- The **direct pathway** helps **start** movements you want.
- The **indirect pathway** **suppresses** movements you don't want.

Dopamine from the substantia nigra tunes both. Faults on either side cause opposite problems:

- **Too little "go":** in [[Parkinson's disease|parkinsons-disease]], dopamine cells in the substantia nigra die. Starting movements becomes hard and slow, and there's a tremor at rest that lessens with movement. [[L-DOPA|l-dopa]] helps the surviving cells make more dopamine but can't replace lost cells.
- **Too little "stop":** in [[hemiballismus]], damage to the subthalamic nucleus lets violent, flinging movements break through on the opposite side of the body. In [[Tourette syndrome|tourette-syndrome]], faulty loops let unwanted tics and sounds slip out.

[[Deep brain stimulation|deep-brain-stimulation]] can help several of these. Electrodes placed in a deep target deliver steady pulses. How it works is still debated; it may override abnormal activity rather than simply boost it.

## The cerebellum {#cerebellum}

::diagram{id="brain-midsagittal" highlight="cerebellum" caption="The cerebellum sits behind the brainstem."}

The [[cerebellum]] holds about 70–80% of the brain's neurons. It helps select, plan and correct movements, and it's where practice turns into skill ([[motor learning|motor-learning]]).

Most of its input comes from the body senses. It compares where your limbs actually are with where you intended them to be, combines that with other senses, and sends corrections to the motor cortex. Repetition tunes those corrections, which is how a skill becomes smooth. If you wear prism glasses that shift everything sideways, you miss a dart target at first, then adapt within a few throws; people with cerebellar damage don't adapt.

[[Damage to the cerebellum|cerebellar-ataxia]], or a few drinks of alcohol, makes movement clumsy:

- **Dyssynergia:** a movement that should be smooth breaks into jerky pieces.
- **Dysmetria:** movements overshoot or undershoot, such as missing your nose in a finger-to-nose test.
- A tremor **during** movement, unlike the resting tremor of Parkinson's disease.
